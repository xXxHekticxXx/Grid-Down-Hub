# Florida Hazards Content (verified 2026-10-26)

## Hurricanes: evacuate or stay?
*Source: [FloridaDisaster.org Know Your Zone](https://www.floridadisaster.org/planprepare/make-an-evacuation-plan/know-your-zone-know-your-home/) · [Disaster Preparedness Maps](https://www.floridadisaster.org/planprepare/disaster-preparedness-maps/)*

- The biggest hurricane killer is **storm surge flooding**. Inland counties around Orlando don't have surge evacuation zones.
- **If you live in a mobile home,** an evacuation zone, a low-lying flood-prone area or an unsafe structure, **follow all evacuation orders.** Orange County has issued mandatory evacuations specifically for mobile-home residents before, for Hurricane Irma in 2017 ([Orange County](https://newsroom.ocfl.net/2017/09/orange-county-issues-mandatory-evacuation-notice-residents-mobile-homes-due-expected-impact/)).
- **Frank's note for Kyle:** a camper is not a safe place to ride out a hurricane. Treat any hurricane warning as "leave." Pre-plan where you and the dog will go (a pet-friendly shelter, friends or family, or a solid building), and save it in **My Plan**.
- **Lake County (Kyle's county)** says manufactured-home residents should **always shelter in a safe site-built structure** for hurricanes. Pet-friendly shelters are in `08`.
- Always follow your county emergency management. App feature: a "Before the storm" checklist, plus an editable list of shelter locations that Kyle updates each June.

## Flooding
*Source: [NWS Turn Around Don't Drown](https://www.weather.gov/safety/flood-turn-around-dont-drown)*
- **6 inches** of fast-moving water can knock an adult down.
- **12 inches** of rushing water carries away most cars.
- **2 feet** carries away SUVs and trucks.
- Most flood drownings happen in vehicles. **Never drive or walk into flood water.** The road under it may be gone. If your vehicle stalls in rising water, get out and get to higher ground ([NOAA](https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/thunderstorms/flood)).

## Lightning
*Source: [NWS Lightning Tips](https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-tips)*
- **When thunder roars, go indoors.** If you can hear thunder, lightning can reach you. **No place outside is safe.**
- Safe shelter: a substantial building with plumbing or electricity, or a hard-topped metal vehicle with the windows up. Sheds, tents and open vehicles are **not** safe.
- **Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder** before going back out.
- Indoors: stay off corded phones and plugged-in electronics, stay away from plumbing and windows, and don't lie on or lean against concrete.
- Caught outside with no shelter: get off high ground; **never** lie flat or shelter under a lone tree; get away from water and wire fences.

## Generators, grills and carbon monoxide
*Source: [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/response/what-to-do-protect-yourself-during-a-power-outage.html) · [CDC generator flyer](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/media/pdfs/Generators_1.pdf)*
- **Never run a generator, pressure washer or any gas engine inside** a home, garage or camper, even with doors and windows open.
- Keep generators **more than 20 feet** from any window, door or vent. Use an extension cord longer than 20 feet.
- **Never use a charcoal grill, hibachi, lantern or camp stove inside a home, tent or camper.**
- Never heat a home with a gas range or oven. Never idle a vehicle in a closed garage.
- Use a **battery-powered or battery-backup CO detector** near where you sleep.

## Downed power lines and electrical hazards
*Source: [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/response/what-to-do-protect-yourself-during-a-power-outage.html)*
- **Never touch a fallen power line.**
- Don't drive through standing water if lines are down in it.
- If someone may have been shocked, call 911.

## Heat
See `01` (heat stroke and heat exhaustion). Without air conditioning, CDC advises going to friends or a community cooling shelter if it gets too hot ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/response/what-to-do-protect-yourself-during-a-power-outage.html)).

## Florida's six venomous snakes
*Source: [Florida Poison Control](https://floridapoisoncontrol.org/poisoning-in-florida/snakes/) · [Florida Museum](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/florida-snake-id/venomous-snake-statement/)*

| Snake | Where | How to recognize |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eastern (Harlequin) Coral Snake | Statewide except the southern Keys | Red, black and yellow rings; **red touches yellow** (like a stoplight); blunt black nose. Mimics have red touching black |
| Cottonmouth / Water Moccasin | Statewide, near water | 2–4 ft, dark brown or black; white mouth lining when threatened; young ones have rusty bands and a yellow tail tip |
| Eastern Diamondback Rattlesnake | Statewide: pinelands, scrub, even towns | Largest (3–6 ft); diamond pattern with beige borders; rattle; can strike up to 2/3 of its body length |
| Pygmy Rattlesnake | Most of Florida except the Keys | Small (1–2 ft), gray with dark blotches, dark band from eye to jaw; **rattle is faint and hard to hear** |
| Timber (Canebrake) Rattlesnake | North Florida only | Up to 5 ft; reddish-brown back stripe; black tail |
| Copperhead | Small area along the Apalachicola River, north Florida | Copper colored, up to about 3 ft, alternating darker and lighter bands; young have a yellow tail tip |

For Central Florida, the coral snake, cottonmouth, eastern diamondback and pygmy rattlesnake are the relevant ones. Most Florida snakes are not venomous, but any snake can bite. First aid is in `01`.
