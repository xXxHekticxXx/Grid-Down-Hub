## When this applies
Ticks are common in Florida pastures, palmetto scrub and the Ocala National Forest, on you and on the dog. The longer a tick stays attached (especially over 24 to 48 hours), the higher the chance of disease.

## Do this
1. **Remove it as soon as you find it.** Don't wait for a doctor.
2. **Grab the tick as close to the skin as you can with clean, fine-tipped tweezers.**
3. **Pull straight up with steady, even pressure.** Don't twist or jerk. If mouthparts stay behind and won't come out easily, leave them; the skin will push them out.
4. **Clean the bite and your hands** with soap and water or alcohol.
5. **Get rid of the tick:** seal it in tape or a container, or flush it. Don't crush it with fingers.
6. **Check everyone every evening** in the field: scalp, behind the ears, armpits, waistband, groin, behind the knees. Check the dog's ears, neck, armpits and between toes.

## Warning signs: get help
- Rash (especially a spreading red ring, or small spots on wrists and ankles), fever, chills, headache, body aches or fatigue in the days to weeks after a bite: see a doctor and say you had a tick bite.
- A deer tick that was swollen with blood: see a doctor within 72 hours about preventive antibiotics.

## Do NOT
- Do not use petroleum jelly, nail polish, alcohol or a hot match to make it back out.
- Do not squeeze the tick's body.

## What you need
- Fine-tipped tweezers or a tick tool in every kit.
- Permethrin-treated clothing and an EPA-registered repellent; see [Mosquitoes and biting insects](#/a/insect-protection).
- Tick prevention for the dog from your vet.
