## When this applies
Before you eat any wild plant. Wild plants can add vitamins and calories, but they will not feed you alone, and mistakes kill. UF/IFAS warns many edible plants have toxic parts or deadly look-alikes.

## Do this
1. **Be 100% sure.** Match several features (leaf shape and arrangement, stem, flower, fruit, smell, habitat, season), not one. If not certain, don't eat it.
2. **Learn the killers first:** [water hemlock](#/a/toxic-plants-florida) grows in Florida ditches and wetlands and is mistaken for wild carrot; rosary pea, castor bean, oleander, sago palm and coontie seeds, jimsonweed and nightshades.
3. **Know which part is edible** and when. Elderberry flowers and cooked ripe berries are food; the leaves, stems and raw berries make you sick.
4. **Start small:** a little the first time, wait a day.
5. **Gather clean:** away from roads, power lines (herbicide), farm runoff and septic fields; wash in treated water.
6. **The "universal edibility test"** in ATP 3-50.21 chapter 4 is a last-resort military method that takes about a day per plant and still misses some poisons. Use known plants instead.
7. **Foraging on public land:** collecting plants in national forests and state parks may be limited or need a permit; on private land, get permission.

## Warning signs: get help
- Vomiting, sweating, drooling, confusion, seizures or a racing or slow heart after eating a wild plant: **911 and Poison Help 1-800-222-1222.** Bring a sample or photo of the plant.

## Do NOT
- **Do not eat wild mushrooms.** Several deadly Amanita species grow in Florida.
- Do not eat any plant with milky sap, an almond smell, or umbrella-shaped clusters of small white flowers unless you're certain.
- Do not trust "animals eat it so it's safe."

## What you need
- This app's plant pages, photos you take yourself, and a second person to confirm identification.
